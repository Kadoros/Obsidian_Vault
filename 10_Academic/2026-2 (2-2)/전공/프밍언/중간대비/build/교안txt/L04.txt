Introduction
§ Syntax Analysis
    • The syntax analysis portion of a language processor nearly always consists
      of two parts:
      - A low-level part called a lexical analyzer (mathematically, a finite automaton
        based on a regular grammar)
      - A high-level part called a syntax analyzer, or parser (mathematically, a push-down
        automaton based on a context-free grammar, or BNF)




Chung-Ang University CSE 22405: Principles of Programming Languages                          6
Introduction
§ Reasons to Separate Lexical and Syntax Analysis
    • Simplicity (단순성)
      - Less complex approaches can be used for lexical analysis; separating them
        simplifies the parser
    • Efficiency (효율성)
      - Separation allows optimization of the lexical analyzer
    • Portability (이식성)
      - Parts of the lexical analyzer may not be portable, but the parser always is portable

The Parsing Problem (파싱 문제)
§ Parsing (파싱)
    • The process of analyzing a sequence of tokens to determine whether it
      conforms to the syntax (grammar) of a programming language


§ Goals of the parser, given an input program:
    • Find all syntax errors;
      - For each, produce an appropriate diagnostic message and recover quickly
    • Produce the parse tree, or at least a trace of the parse tree, for the program




Chung-Ang University CSE 22405: Principles of Programming Languages                32
The Parsing Problem
§ Two categories of parsers
    • Top down (하향식): produce the parse tree, beginning at the root
      - Order is that of a leftmost derivation
      - Traces or builds the parse tree in preorder (전위순회)
    • Bottom up (상향식): produce the parse tree, beginning at the leaves
      - Order is that of the reverse of a rightmost derivation

§ Depth First Traversals (or Searches):
    • In-order (Left, Root, Right): 4 2 5 1 3
    • Pre-order (Root, Left, Right): 1 2 4 5 3
    • Post-order (Left, Right, Root): 4 5 2 3 1

§ Notational conventions
    • Terminal symbols: lowercase letters at the beginning of the alphabet (a, b,
      …)
    • Nonterminal symbols: uppercase letters at the beginning of the alphabet
      (A, B, …)
    • Terminals and non-terminals: uppercase letters at the end of the
      alphabet (W, X, Y, Z)
    • Strings of terminals: lowercase letters at the end of alphabet (w, x, y, z)
    • Mixed strings (terminals and/or non-terminals): lowercase Greek letters
      (𝛼, 𝛽, 𝛾, 𝛿)

The Parsing Problem
§ The Complexity of Parsing
    • Parsers that work for any unambiguous grammar are complex and
      inefficient (O(𝑛! ), where n is the length of the input )
    • Compilers use parsers that only work for a subset of all unambiguous
      grammars, but do it in linear time (O(𝑛), where n is the length of the input )

Recursive-Descent Parsing
§ A collection of subprograms
    • Recursive
    • Produces a parse tree in top-down order
§ This recursion is a reflection of the nature of programming
  languages, which include several different kinds of nested
  structures.
§ There is a subprogram for each nonterminal in the grammar,
  which can parse sentences that can be generated by that
  nonterminal
§ EBNF is ideally suited for being the basis for a recursive-
  descent parser, because EBNF minimizes the number of
  nonterminals

§ The LL Grammar Class (LL문법 유형)
    • The Left Recursion Problem (좌-순환 문제)
      - If a grammar has left recursion, either direct or indirect, it cannot be the basis for
        a top-down parser
         • A grammar can be modified to remove direct left recursion as follows:


      - For each nonterminal, A,
         • Group the A-rules as
                   A → Aα1 | ... | Aαm | β1 |β2 | ... | βn, where none of the β‘s begins with A
         • Replace the original A-rules with
                     A → β1A’ | β2A’ | ... | βnA’
                     A’→ α1A’ | α2A’ | ... | αmA’ | ε

Bottom-Up Parsing
§ LR parsers are table driven, where the table has two
  components, an ACTION table and a GOTO table
    • The ACTION table speciﬁes the action of the parser, given the parser state
      and the next token
      - Rows are state names; columns are terminals
    • The GOTO table speciﬁes which state to put on top of the parse stack after
      a reduction action is done
      - Rows are state names; columns are nonterminals

§ Parser actions:
    • For a Shift, the next symbol of input is pushed onto the stack, along with

    • For a Reduce, remove the handle from the stack, along with its state

      table, using the state symbol just below the new LHS in the stack and the
      LHS of the new rule as the row and column into the GOTO table
